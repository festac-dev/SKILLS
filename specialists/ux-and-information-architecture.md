# Specialist: UX and Information Architecture

Map real user tasks, content relationships, navigation, journeys, permissions and recovery paths.

Match the requested copy density. Use brief labels and direct status messages; put secondary explanation in optional help. For single-item forms, omit numbering that only distinguishes batch rows. Keep units in amount labels, and show batch controls or secondary instructions when they become relevant. When a creation action is meant to work in the interface, connect it to the actual operation and resulting state; a copied terminal command does not establish that GUI journey. Preserve any genuine runtime limitations and existing-data protections.

Consider landing pages, application shells, headers, footers, sidebars, tabs, command menus, breadcrumbs, dashboards, forms, tables, search, filters, editors, maps, timelines, collaboration and specialist workflows only when relevant.

Do not force any pattern. Test labels, hierarchy, discoverability, responsive behavior, keyboard use and error recovery. Optimize for task completion and comprehension, not novelty.

## Applied verification

Walk the primary task from the landing action through a populated workspace and back. Remove labels that do not help users choose a destination; test deep links, active navigation, mobile menus and recovery without relying on a tour.

For template changes, inventory the existing destinations and meaningful states before replacing markup. Choose the navigation and reading sequence around the product's main task; preserve a coherent path from the new introduction into the existing workspace. A different hero illustration alone does not establish a different information architecture.

## Reference-led redesign and workspace continuity

Map each public promise to a real destination, then choose the workspace around its principal decision: inspect, compare, compose, approve, monitor or recover. A marketing hero's density and motion do not automatically belong in the operating surface. Preserve selected records, filters, deep links, permissions and pending work through navigation changes.

Review a complete populated flow across list, detail, action and result. Treat attractive reference screens as proposed states; they do not establish validation, payment, saving or authorization behavior. On narrow screens, decide content order and action placement explicitly. Large scenic areas should still leave an obvious way into the task.

For icon-only controls requested by the brief, retain an accessible name, state and clear focus treatment. Place common controls consistently, but do not hide essential labels solely to imitate a reference. Verify actual positions and hit areas in the rendered interface, including wrapped headers and mobile navigation.

For carousels and galleries, choose the pattern from the content and comparison task. Preserve item order, selected state, keyboard navigation, touch access and a useful static layout. Do not put essential actions or important information exclusively on an auto-advancing slide. Perspective, drag and looping are optional treatments; test reflow, focus and interruption before treating a carousel as finished.

Translate mobile references into complete transitions: list to detail, selection to confirmation, validation to recovery, and return to the same context. Choose persistent navigation from the frequency of real destinations and available screen area. Do not merely shrink a desktop table or add a bottom bar with duplicate destinations. Check safe areas, scrolling containers, focus after navigation and touch targets with populated content.

When a landing gallery previews the product, use actual authorized captures or clearly labeled reconstructions. Link each preview to the corresponding working destination. Avoid private records and unsupported success states. Keep captions useful at the image's rendered size, and provide the same destination without requiring a drag or animation to finish.

For dense records on narrow screens, preserve the value/action relationship. A wide table may need deliberate horizontal scrolling or a labeled list/card presentation; do not squeeze identifiers, amounts and export links into unreadable fragments. Check detail views too: grid/flex children and raw-data panels need shrinkable widths, long tokens need wrapping, and the user needs a visible return path. Active navigation should follow the detail’s owning section.

Keep simulation, fault injection and destructive demo probes out of everyday task actions. When they belong to the brief, place them in a clearly labeled testing area and explain their actual effect before execution. Preserve server-side invariants; hiding an action is an information-architecture choice, not an authorization boundary.

For a requested automatic batch or participant plan, review the overall amount, destination, execution mode and meaningful limits once. Bind later actions to that approval at the service boundary and recheck changing inputs before each action; do not turn stale browser allocations into repeated approval prompts. Persist progress, prevent duplicate uncertain submissions, and distinguish stopping future work from cancelling work already submitted. Keep detailed progress in its owning task pages instead of repeating a large waiting panel on unrelated pages. Test the third item, not only the first two, as well as changed capacity, restart and stop.

For editable currency conversions, identify the quote source and freshness, show fees separately and preserve the provider's integer precision or amount quantum. Make either-side entry update the other side without silently submitting a stale valid amount after invalid input. An estimate is not a guarantee against market loss. Test a nontrivial rate whose exact provider amount differs from floating-point division, and recheck terms before approval.

When users report that a button does nothing, inspect the request, the actual error and where feedback appears at their current scroll position. Show progress while the request runs and keep a failure near its triggering action; a banner above the viewport can be missed. Renew expiring demo or cached data through the owning lifecycle, rather than requiring a manual refresh to make an otherwise valid action work.

Distinguish total funds from commitments and free funds when workflows share a balance. An open reservation need not block unrelated work when the unreserved balance is sufficient. Recheck shared commitments at execution and avoid counting the released portion of a reservation twice.

Separate the validity of stored receiving data from permission to perform a payment. Where appropriate, a demo can store real, validated destinations while remaining unable to fund them. Identify address networks, validate them again before a live transaction, and show local save feedback. Optional names can use clear automatic labels instead of leaving a valid-address form silently disabled.

Make deletion of saved entries easy to find beside each entry, using a short action label. Show progress and failures at that row, keep failed entries visible, and confirm a successful removal with a brief status. Verify that deletion persists after reload and that other entries remain. Deleting a saved destination removes that saved record; ownership of its underlying account or wallet follows the separate resource-management contract.

Separate preparing a review from executing its action. When execution must wait, let a valid review open and explain the blocked confirmation there. Scope settings restrictions to the changes that affect active work, report save failures beside Save, and populate limits and unit hints from the saved settings. Preserve approved terms already attached to an active operation; apply changed defaults to new reviews.
